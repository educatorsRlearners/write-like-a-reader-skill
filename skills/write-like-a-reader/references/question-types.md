# Question types

A good question presses on the writer's claim and asks the writer to say more. It is never a factual lookup and never a restatement of the sentence.

The sentence: "Cell phones have changed the way people communicate."

- **Good:** "How have cell phones changed the way people communicate?" The claim is vague. The question asks the writer to explain it.
- **Bad:** "What has changed the way people communicate?" The sentence already answers it: cell phones.
- **Bad:** "When were cell phones invented?" The answer is a fact. The gap lies in the writer's explanation, not in a missing date.

Before you keep a question that starts with "what" or "who," check it against the rule. It must ask for the writer's explanation, not for a fact or for something the sentence has already named.

## The forms

Each form below shows the question, a gap of that kind in a draft, and the lightest fix.

### How

Asks in what way, or by what mechanism.

- Claim: "Remote work improves focus."
- Question: How does it improve focus?
- Gap: a result stated with no process behind it.
- Fix: add the mechanism in a clause ("because nobody interrupts you at a desk").

### Why

Asks for the reason or cause.

- Claim: "We chose Postgres."
- Question: Why Postgres?
- Gap: a decision with no reasoning.
- Fix: give the one reason that decided it.

### To what extent

Asks how much, how often, or for whom.

- Claim: "The change cut load times."
- Question: By how much, and for which pages?
- Gap: a claim that could mean anything from 1% to 90%.
- Fix: give the size and the scope, or narrow the claim ("cut load times on the home page").

### Under what conditions

Asks when the claim holds and when it fails.

- Claim: "Caching speeds up the service."
- Question: When does it not?
- Gap: a universal claim that has exceptions the reader will think of.
- Fix: state the condition ("for read-heavy endpoints").

### For example

Asks for a concrete case.

- Claim: "Good names reduce bugs."
- Question: Can you show one?
- Gap: an abstraction with nothing to hold.
- Fix: add a short, specific example. One is enough.

### Compared to what

Asks for the baseline or the alternative.

- Claim: "The new build is fast."
- Question: Faster than what?
- Gap: a comparative with no second term.
- Fix: name the baseline, or drop the comparison.

### So what

Asks for the consequence.

- Claim: "Support tickets doubled in March."
- Question: Why does this matter?
- Gap: a finding that stops short of its meaning.
- Fix: add the consequence in the same sentence or the next.

### What follows

Asks what the reader should do or conclude.

- Claim: "The vendor's contract expires in June."
- Question: What do we do about it?
- Gap: information with no next step.
- Fix: state the recommendation or the decision needed.

### How do you know

Asks for the support behind a claim.

- Claim: "Most users prefer the dark theme."
- Question: What is that based on?
- Gap: an assertion with no evidence.
- Fix: cite the evidence you have. If you have none, cut or soften the claim. Never invent a source.

## Triage

Answer first the questions that block belief or action. A reader who cannot believe a claim, or cannot act on a recommendation, stops reading.

Ignore the rest:

- questions whose answers are obvious to this reader;
- questions that pull the text off its topic;
- questions the document answers in the next sentence;
- questions that would need a paragraph to answer and deserve a clause at most.

When a question needs a fact that is not in the project, the conversation, or command output, do not supply it from memory. If the claim is yours, ask the user one plain question, or cut or soften the claim. If the claim is the user's, leave it in the text. Tell the user in one sentence which claim lacks support and what fact would support it.
